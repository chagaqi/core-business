# Lead-Magnet Nurture Sequence

**Goes to:** List A only. Express opt-in, documented per contact (they downloaded the Template Pack). Sent from the Resend marketing subdomain, never the cold domains. A cold-outreach reply never auto-joins this list. (CASL two-lists rule, GTM plan §9.2.)

**Shape:** 6 emails over ~14 days, then a monthly check-in. The buyer is a flow, not a stock. Their pain has an end date, so the sequence resolves fast toward one ask: a private pilot, never a self-serve signup.

**Terminal CTA everywhere:** reply to book a 15-minute teardown. A pilot-CTA click exits the sequence to the booked call.

**Voice:** the operator who ran the where's-my-order inbox. Proof-only. No fabricated metrics or track record. Anti-slop pass on every send (`copy-standard.md`). Bracketed fields are the only per-send edits.

>
> — Dylan

**Why:** A drowning founder wants text to paste today, not a welcome essay. Point them at the one template for their current fire. The value lands in the first sixty seconds, which earns the next five emails.

## Email 1 — Day 2 — The silence is the wound, not the wait

**Subject:** The wait isn't what gets you the chargeback

> Quick one, because it's the thing I wish someone had told me early.
>
> Backers don't dispute because the wait is long. They dispute because the wait goes *quiet*. Silence gets filled with the worst assumption they've got, usually "I've been scammed," and once that hardens it's a card dispute or a public post.
>
> The fix is stupidly cheap: a ten-second update with one concrete detail, sent before they have to ask. "Production photos came in this week, still on track for the [X]-week window." That's it. That resets their anxiety clock.
>
> Template 7 in the pack is the proof-of-life update. Send it to your quietest backers this week, the ones you *haven't* heard from. Those are the ones drifting toward a dispute in silence.
>
> — Dylan

**Why:** One insight, made concrete, tied to a specific template and a specific action they can take today. Teaches the single highest-leverage habit in fulfillment comms and proves you know the job.

## Email 2 — Day 5 — The founder story, as proof

**Subject:** Why I built this

> I ran fulfillment and the where's-my-order inbox for a physical-goods brand for a couple of years. We did $200K in our first week once and then spent 60-plus days making people wait for it, twice over.

> I answered the day-60 refund email at 11pm more times than I can count. I learned every one of these templates the hard way, by getting the wording wrong first and watching a backer go to their bank over it.

> That's the whole reason the pack exists, and it's the reason I'm building **Tideover**: software that reads where each backer is in the wait and how upset they are, drafts the right one of these messages, flags who's about to bail so you answer them first, and never lets a hard date or a fake tracking number reach a customer. You approve and send.
>
> It's the pack, running itself, on every ticket. If that sounds like the week you're having, hit reply.
>
> — Dylan

**Why:** Day 5 is where you convert credibility into consideration. The real story is the proof, and it's the one thing a funded competitor can't copy. The product intro is one paragraph, earned by the story, not pitched cold.

## Email 3 — Day 8 — The objection they haven't said yet

**Subject:** "Won't an automated reply sound like a robot?"

> The fear I'd have about software touching my backer inbox: it sends something generic and cold and makes the wait *worse*.
>
> Fair. So here's the line Tideover holds. It never invents a fact. No tracking number until a real one exists. No "on schedule" when nobody controls the schedule. No hard date, ever, because a date you miss is the screenshot in a dispute. It gives an ETA as a band and it says what's actually true about the order, which is exactly what you'd do on your best day, at 2pm, before the inbox wore you down.
>
> And you approve every message before it sends. The software does the reading and the drafting. You keep the judgment.
>
> That's the difference between automation that helps and automation that embarrasses you. Want to see it run on your actual tickets? 15 minutes, reply and I'll set it up.
>
> — Dylan

**Why:** Handle the real objection to the product (automated comms feel robotic and risky) before it calcifies into a no. The answer is the proof-only doctrine, which is genuinely differentiating. First soft ask for the call.

## Email 4 — Day 11 — The private pilot ask

**Subject:** Want me to set this up with you personally?

> Straight ask.
>
> I'm setting up a few crowdfunding and preorder brands on Tideover myself, by hand, right now. Not a signup form. Me, on a call, wiring it to your actual backer inbox and your real timelines, and watching the first batch of drafts with you.
>
> If you're mid-fulfillment and the where's-my-order load is real, this is the moment it's worth 15 minutes. I'll show you where in your wait your backers are most likely to bail, and what the software would say to them.
>
> Reply with a couple of times that work, or just "tell me more." Either's fine.
>
> — Dylan

**Why:** The terminal CTA, stated plainly. A private, hands-on pilot matches both the buyer (a stressed founder who wants a person, not a dashboard) and the bandwidth (you can only take a few). The ask is low-friction: two times, or "tell me more."

## Email 5 — Day 14 — Last nudge, for the ones who didn't click

**Subject:** Closing the loop

> Last one on this for now, so I'm not another unread sender in an inbox that's already too full.
>
> If the Template Pack helped, that was the point. Keep it, use it, no strings.
>
> If the wait's genuinely eating your week and you want a hand with it, the teardown offer stands. 15 minutes, your real tickets, and you'll walk away with the two or three points in your fulfillment where backers are most likely to bail, whether or not you ever use the software.
>
> Reply anytime. I'll switch you to my once-a-month note after this so I'm not in your way.
>
> — Dylan

**Why:** A clean last touch that respects the inbox and re-states the standalone value of the call (they get the weak-points map regardless). "I'll switch you to monthly" is a genuine promise that makes the unsubscribe unnecessary and keeps them warm for a later cycle.

## Monthly check-in — after the sequence, ongoing

**Subject:** [one specific, useful thing] — the monthly note

> One useful thing this month: [a specific, concrete item. A new template added to the pack. A fulfillment stat worth knowing, cited. A pattern from a real teardown, anonymized, never a named struggling creator.]
>
> That's it. If you're back in a fulfillment crunch, the teardown offer's always open. Reply.
>
> — Dylan

**Why:** The buyer is a flow. A backer's wait ends, but new campaigns launch constantly, and the founder who downloaded the pack in month one may be mid-crunch again in month six. One concrete useful thing a month keeps you the person they think of when it's on fire again. Never a named struggling creator: they're the customer, not the content.

## Sending notes

- **Automations, not broadcasts, for emails 0–5** (Resend Automations, shipped April 2026: event-triggered, delays, branching). The monthly note is a broadcast.
- **Branch on the pilot click:** anyone who books or replies "yes" exits the sequence immediately. Don't send Email 5's "last nudge" to someone already on your calendar.
- **Every send gets the anti-slop pass** and the proof-only gate, same as the product. No fabricated metrics. No "honest." No hard dates, even here.
- **CASL:** every send carries sender identification and a working one-click unsubscribe, honored promptly. This list is express-consent only.
