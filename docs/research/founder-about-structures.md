## 1) The structural arc that wins

Convergent across the strongest pages (Basecamp, ConvertKit, Ugmonk, Plausible):

1. **Lived problem, first person, in scene.** Not "the industry has a problem." Basecamp opens with a design firm drowning: "Quality was suffering, and our clients noticed before we did" (https://basecamp.com/about). Ugmonk opens with a constraint: couldn't find the shirts he wanted (https://ugmonk.com/pages/about).
2. **The bottom / failed alternatives.** The low point is named, not skipped. Nathan Barry names $1,207 MRR as the floor and "our personal accounts were the lowest they'd been in years" (https://nathanbarry.com/5k/).
3. **The insight, one sentence.** Basecamp: "If we need it, surely others need it." Ahrefs: "The AI marketing platform we needed didn't exist. So we built it." (https://ahrefs.com/about).
4. **What we believe, stated as mechanisms, not adjectives.** Plausible: "We do not use cookies and we do not collect personal data," with links to the policy docs as proof (https://plausible.io/about). Linear names the "dark forces" it opposes before pitching itself (https://linear.app/readme).
5. **Honest limits, the trust unlock.** Plausible flatly says it is "not the right tool for every use case" and "customers are not locked in… the code can be forked" (https://plausible.io/about).
6. **Invitation.** Low-pressure CTA plus direct contact. Basecamp ends with try-it + reach-me-directly; Linear recruits the reader into the mission ("That person is you") (https://linear.app/readme).

## 2) Tone rules

- **First person singular beats corporate "we" for solo founders.** The most trustworthy pages are one named human: Jason Fried, Jeff Sheldon, Nathan Barry. Ugmonk's "I'm a designer by trade, an entrepreneur by accident" does more credibility work than any logo wall (https://ugmonk.com/pages/about).
- **Humility is load-bearing, not decorative.** Basecamp admits "desperate necessity" and declines to claim genius ("I wish I could credit a spark") (https://basecamp.com/about). Barry admits giving advice he then ignored: "Despite giving that advice I made the exact same mistake" (https://nathanbarry.com/5k/).
- **Specificity is the anti-slop mechanism.** Dates, dollar figures, named low points. Barry's rule survives as copy doctrine: "If your market definition has the word 'anyone' in it, you'll probably fail" (https://nathanbarry.com/5k/).
- **Short paragraphs, mixed sentence length, one rhetorical question max** ("Sound familiar?", Basecamp).

## 3) Length + formatting norms

- Founder-letter about pages run **800-1,200 words** (Basecamp ~800, Ugmonk ~900, Plausible ~1,200). Manifestos run 2,000-2,500 but need a design system to carry them (Linear). A 400-550-word letter is at the tight end; fine, provided the arc stays complete rather than compressing every beat equally.
- **Prose-first, minimal headers, generous whitespace.** Bullets only where mechanisms are listed (Plausible's value props, PostHog's differentiators).
- Sign-off with a first name and direct contact is a recurring founder-letter convention.

## 4) How numbers are woven in without bragging

- **Numbers as scene-setting or diagnosis, never trophies.** Barry's numbers explain the shape of the curve ($1,207 → $4,067), and he discloses the $50,000 he had to invest so the win can't read as effortless (https://nathanbarry.com/5k/).
- **Deliberate vagueness where precision would boast.** Basecamp: "tens of thousands" of users after 23 years; the humility is the message (https://basecamp.com/about).
- **Proof by verifiable mechanism, not assertion.** Plausible: "That kind of trust cannot be bought," backed by open-source code and linked policies a skeptic can check (https://plausible.io/about). This is the exact template for Tideover's proof-only doctrine.
- **Big numbers must be checkable and oddly specific.** PostHog's "190,254+ customers" and Ahrefs' crawl stats work because they are precise and externally verifiable (https://posthog.com/about, https://ahrefs.com/about).

## Draft v1 for Dylan (~475 words, verbatim from the research pass)

# Why I built Tideover

In 2020 I started a gym equipment business the week the gyms closed. I closed $200K in orders in my first seven days. That sounds like a win. It was the start of the hardest two years of my working life.

Every one of those orders shipped into the worst freight delays in e-commerce history. Customers waited 60 days and more, and every morning the inbox refilled with the same question: where is my order? I couldn't make a single container move faster. The only real job was convincing good people to wait a little longer, then doing it again the next day.

I used to think: if I could wave a magic wand and the stress of handling the onslaught of inquiries — when you know there's nothing you can do but convince them to wait a bit longer — could just go away in an instant, it would have saved me my sanity, my nervous system, my family, and my time to focus on growing the business.

That's where Tideover was born. The name is literal. I spent two years tiding customers over, and I learned, order by order and refund by refund, what keeps a customer calm through a long wait and what turns a delay into a chargeback.

Here's the honest part: you shouldn't have to learn it the way I did. Instinct doesn't scale. At 11pm, on your three-hundredth "where is my order" email, instinct fails. So I built the judgment into software instead of leaving it to nerves:

- Every reply Tideover drafts is audited in code before it reaches a customer.
- Hard delivery dates you can't control are physically blocked. The system will not send a promise you can't keep.
- Results are measured against one thing only: your own store's baseline. Never a benchmark I invented.

One pledge governs everything on this site: proof only. No fabricated metrics, no fake logo wall, no "merchants love us" line I can't back. The numbers above ($200K, 60+ days, two years) are my own account of my own business. They stay the only numbers here until real merchant results, shared with permission, replace them.

Tideover exists for Shopify merchants staring down a 60-120 day fulfillment wait, usually right after a crowdfunding campaign worked better than expected. If that's you, I'm taking on a small group of founding partners. You get me directly, you shape the product, and your results, only with your sign-off, become the proof this page runs on.

If you're in the middle of the onslaught right now, I know exactly where you're standing. Write to me.

— Dylan

## Where Dylan's morning details slot in (3 options)

- **The named bottom (paragraph 2).** The arc's strongest beat is currently the draft's most generic. One specific worst-day scene would complete it: a date, an inbox count, one customer he still remembers. Barry names $1,207 MRR as his floor; Dylan's equivalent ("the night I answered X emails," "the week Y containers sat at Long Beach") turns the WISMO onslaught from claim into scene.
- **The scar-tissue credentials (paragraph 4, "order by order and refund by refund").** Outcome numbers from the two years, if he has them: total orders shipped, refunds issued vs. saved, chargebacks fought or avoided, the goodwill gesture that worked best. Even one number here ("we kept the chargeback count to N across thousands of delayed orders") upgrades "I learned" into checkable diagnosis, PostHog-style odd precision.
- **The honest ending (paragraph 1 or the close).** What happened to the store: sold, wound down when gyms reopened, still running. Naming the ending is the humility beat (Basecamp's move) and preempts the skeptic's first Google: "so where's this gym business now?" If the ending is unglamorous, that strengthens the letter; the business isn't the pitch, the scar tissue is.
